Appendix F

Reference Numbers (dated)

Compiled August 2026. Every volatile number the book leans on is collected here so that the book ages in one appendix rather than in forty-two chapters. Sections F.1 through F.4 hold numbers that drift — accelerator specifications, model cards, prices, benchmark scores. Section F.5 holds the formulas, which do not, and F.6 says how to rebuild the rest. That separation is the point of the appendix: when a chapter says an H100 executes about 295 bf16 FLOPs per byte of HBM traffic, the durable content is the definition of machine balance $B^{*} = \pi/\beta$ from Section 30.3, and the 295 is a perishable input. Re-derive, do not re-read. Where a number here disagrees with a chapter, this appendix is the later measurement and wins; one such disagreement is flagged explicitly in F.1.

Common Pitfall

Do not cite this appendix. Cite the source it names. Accelerator datasheets are revised silently, model cards are edited after release, API prices in this table have moved within a single week, and benchmark leaderboards are re-scored under new harnesses. Every row below carries a Verify tag and names where to check it. A row without a source is a row that was omitted — several were. An incomplete honest table beats a complete invented one, and the failure mode this appendix exists to prevent is a reader quoting a 2026 price in 2028 because it was typeset in a book.

F.1.1 The derived machine balance point

The final column is not a specification. It is $B^{*} = \pi/\beta$, peak dense bf16 FLOP/s divided by HBM bytes per second — Definition 30.2. It is the only number in the table that matters directly to a kernel author, because by Theorem 30.1 an operation with arithmetic intensity $I < B^{*}$ cannot reach peak no matter how the kernel is written. Worked once, for the H100: $989 \times 10^{12} / (3.35 \times 10^{12}) = 295.2$.

Discrepancy with Chapter 30. The hardware table in Section 30.8 lists the B200 at “180–192 GB” and “$\sim$8 TB/s”, giving $B^{*} \approx 281$. Re-verification for this appendix returns 180 GB at 7.7 TB/s, so $2250/7.7 = 292$. The chapter's arithmetic is right for its input; the input conflated two SKUs — the HGX B200 board is 180 GB at 7.7 TB/s, while the 8 TB/s figure belongs to the GB200 NVL package at 186 GB. Use 292 for a B200. Verify This is precisely the drift the appendix exists to absorb, and it is worth noticing how small it is: a 4% error in a bandwidth spec moved the balance point by 4% and changed no conclusion in the chapter. The conclusions that depend on $B^{*}$ — that batch-1 decode sits two to three orders of magnitude below it, that elementwise kernels never approach it — survive any plausible revision of these rows.

Two structural facts the table encodes are more durable than any cell in it. First, $B^{*}$ has stayed inside a band of roughly 150 to 320 across all five accelerators above and both vendors, because FLOP/s and bandwidth have scaled together by design; a hypothetical accelerator with $B^{*} = 30$ would make most of Part VI moot, and none exists. Second, the H200 is an H100 with a better memory system and an unchanged compute peak — it lowers $B^{*}$ from 295 to 206 and nothing else — which is the cleanest available evidence that decode is bandwidth-bound in the market's own judgement. F.5 Formulas that do not drift

Everything above expires. Nothing below does. These are the closed forms the book uses to convert hardware and model numbers into decisions, and each is stated with the caveat that makes it wrong when ignored.

F.5.1 Training compute: $C \approx 6ND$

Training a dense transformer of $N$ parameters on $D$ tokens costs approximately $C = 6ND$ FLOPs: two FLOPs per parameter per token for the forward multiply–accumulate, and about twice that again for the backward pass, which computes gradients with respect to both activations and weights. The neglected term is attention's score and value products: $4nd$ FLOPs per token per layer before masking, halved to about $2nd$ by causality since a token attends to $n/2$ positions on average, against the $24d^2$ of the parameter matrices — a ratio of $n/(12d)$, as Section 24.2.1 derives it. At $d = 4096$ the correction stays under 10% for $n$ below about 4,900 tokens and becomes the dominant term long before $n$ reaches a modern context window. Use $6ND$ for budget arithmetic and scaling-law fits as in Chapter 24; do not use it for long-context runs without adding the quadratic term. Certain

F.5.2 Inference compute: $2N$ per token

One forward pass costs about $2N$ FLOPs per token, the same multiply–accumulate count without the backward factor of three. For a mixture-of-experts model substitute the active parameter count: DeepSeek-V3 costs $2 \times 37\text{B} = 74$ GFLOP per token, not $2 \times 671$B. The memory system does not get the same discount — every expert must be resident because the next token may route anywhere — which is the whole content of Section 22.8 and the reason F.2 tabulates both columns. Combined with F.5.1, the inference-aware scaling objective of Section 24.5 is $6ND + 2ND_{\text{inf}}$, whose minimizer sits strictly left of Chinchilla's. Certain

F.5.3 KV-cache size

For $L$ layers, $n_{\text{kv}}$ key/value heads, head dimension $d_h$, and $b$ bytes per element, one sequence of length $T$ occupies $$K(T) \;=\; 2\,L\,n_{\text{kv}}\,d_h\,b\,T \ \text{bytes},$$ the leading 2 being keys plus values. Section 33.2's worked case — $L=32$, $n_{\text{kv}}=8$, $d_h=128$, fp16 — gives $2 \times 32 \times 8 \times 128 \times 2 = 131{,}072$ bytes per token, exactly 128 KiB, so an 8,192-token sequence carries 1 GiB of cache. Multi-query attention is $n_{\text{kv}} = 1$; multi-head is $n_{\text{kv}} = $ the query head count. The formula is exact for standard attention and does not apply to multi-head latent attention, which caches a compressed joint representation instead. Certain

F.5.4 Arithmetic intensity, machine balance, roofline

For an operation performing $C$ FLOPs while moving $Q$ bytes to and from the memory level under consideration, the arithmetic intensity is $I = C/Q$. Given peak throughput $\pi$ FLOP/s and bandwidth $\beta$ bytes/s, the machine balance is $B^{*} = \pi/\beta$ and the attainable rate is bounded by $$P \;\le\; \min(\pi,\ \beta I),$$ so the operation is memory-bound exactly when $I < B^{*}$. Two traps: $Q$ is traffic at a specified level, so an operation memory-bound against HBM may be compute-bound against L2; and $\pi$ must be the peak for the precision actually issued, which is why the dense-versus-sparse distinction in F.1 changes conclusions rather than decorating them. Square bf16 matmul has $I \approx n/3$, giving the compute-bound threshold $n \ge 3B^{*}$; batch-1 decode has $I \approx 1$ regardless of model size. Certain

F.5.5 Ring all-reduce volume

Over $P$ workers all-reducing $S$ bytes, the ring algorithm runs a reduce-scatter and an all-gather, each $P-1$ steps of $S/P$ bytes, so each worker transmits $$V \;=\; 2\,\frac{P-1}{P}\,S \ \text{bytes}, \qquad t \;=\; 2(P-1)\alpha \;+\; 2\,\frac{P-1}{P}\,S\beta$$ in the $\alpha$–$\beta$ model. This is bandwidth-optimal (Theorem 32.1). The bytes saturate at $2S$ as $P$ grows — going from $P=8$ to $P=8000$ increases per-worker traffic by about 14% — while the latency term grows linearly in $P$, which is why rings win on large tensors and lose on small ones, and why hierarchical and tree collectives exist. Certain

F.5.6 The pass@$k$ estimator

For an i.i.d. sampler with per-problem success probability $p$, $\text{pass@}k = 1 - (1-p)^k$. Given $n \ge k$ samples of which $c$ verify correct, the unbiased estimator (Chen et al., 2021) is $$\widehat{\text{pass@}k} \;=\; 1 - \frac{\binom{n-c}{k}}{\binom{n}{k}} \;=\; 1 - \prod_{j=0}^{k-1}\frac{n-c-j}{n-j},$$ and the product form is the one to implement: the binomial coefficients overflow for the $n$ used in coverage studies. The plug-in alternative $1-(1-c/n)^k$ is biased downward — $1-(1-p)^k$ is concave in $p$, so Jensen's inequality puts $\mathbb{E}[1-(1-c/n)^k] \le 1-(1-p)^k$ — and the bias grows as $k$ approaches $n$: at $p = 0.05$, $n = 64$, $k = 32$ it is $-0.08$ against a true value of $0.81$, exactly the regime where pass@$k$ inversion claims between base and RL-tuned models are decided. Section 28.3 also insists on the interpretive point: pass@$k$ at large $k$ measures the sampler's support, not its reliability. Certain

F.5.7 CKKS depth accounting

Each ciphertext–ciphertext multiplication consumes one level from a fixed budget, so a circuit of multiplicative depth $d$ requires at least $d$ levels and refreshing the budget requires bootstrapping. Summing the per-layer ledger of Section 38.4 over $L$ layers gives the per-token depth $d_{\text{tok}}$; for the twelve-layer geometry of Section 41.4, twenty-one levels per block gives $d_{\text{tok}} = 252$, and with $B = 18$ usable levels that is $252/18 = 14$ bootstraps per token. Over $T$ tokens the total depth is $D = T\,d_{\text{tok}}$ and greedy scheduling performs $$\left\lceil \frac{D}{B} \right\rceil - 1 \ \text{bootstraps}$$ by Theorem 41.1 — $\lceil 258{,}048/18 \rceil - 1 = 14{,}335$ for $T = 1024$. Two companions. A ciphertext at level $\ell$ in RNS with $w$-byte limbs occupies $2N\ell w$ bytes, which is why an encrypted KV cache runs two orders of magnitude above its fp16 counterpart. And baby-step–giant-step reduces the Halevi–Shoup diagonal matvec from $d-1$ rotations to $n_1 + n_2 - 2 \approx 2\sqrt{d}$ — 4,095 down to 126 at $d = 4096$ (Theorem 38.1). Depth is the currency; rotations are the tax. Certain

F.6 Re-verifying this appendix

The tables above were built by fetching primary sources, not by recall. Rebuilding them takes under an hour. Fetch the vendor datasheets for F.1 and recompute $B^{*}$ yourself rather than trusting a quoted balance point; pull the model cards and technical reports named in F.2 by their arXiv identifiers; fetch the provider pricing pages for F.3 on the day you need them; and for F.4 read the protocol section of each paper before copying its number.

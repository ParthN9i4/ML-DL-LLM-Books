Appendix E

Reading Crosswalk

The front matter’s instruction — “read the fundamentals in at least two of them” — is worthless without a table saying which two. This appendix supplies one: a row for each of the book’s 42 chapters naming a source for the derivation and a second for the intuition or the working implementation, and marking plainly where no second source exists. Every chapter number and title below was extracted mechanically from the assembled book, not recalled. One rule governs the citations: they name topics, never chapter numbers in other books. Editions repaginate, courses renumber lectures between offerings, and a free PDF revised twice a year does not hold its section numbers still. A pointer to “Goodfellow’s numerical-computation material” survives that; a numbered pointer would be a guess dressed as a citation.

E.1 How to Read Two Sources at Once

Parallel reading fails when both sources are asked to do the same job: two textbook derivations of gradient descent give you one derivation and a wasted afternoon. The method that works assigns each source a distinct role before you open it.

E.1.1 Three roles, three sources

The derivation source proves the thing: assumptions explicit, theorem in a form you could cite, no skipped step. For Parts I–III this is almost always Goodfellow, Bishop, Hastie et al., or Murphy; for Parts V–VI there is frequently no book at all, and the role falls to CS336 or to a primary paper in Appendix D.

The intuition source makes it picturable. Prince is strongest here in print — this book carries no diagrams by design, and Prince’s are the ones to hold in your head. Trask covers anything with a backward pass, Serrano the classical algorithms of Part II, Karpathy anything you will eventually type.

The book’s artifact is the arbiter. When the two appear to disagree you do not adjudicate by authority or publication date: you run the chapter’s artifact, instrument the disputed quantity, and read the number. Appendix C indexes all 42 by what each verifies itself against, which is also an index of what each is competent to arbitrate.

E.1.2 Most disagreements are notational

Before treating a conflict as substantive, apply one test: does the numeric prediction differ? If it does not, you have found a convention, not a contradiction. The usual suspects are row versus column vectors (this book fixes column vectors and $y = Wx$; several sources use $x^{\top}W$), the factor of $\tfrac{1}{2}$ in a squared loss, natural log versus $\log_2$ in every information-theoretic quantity, whether a fused multiply–add counts as one FLOP or two (Chapter 1 counts two, giving $2mnk$; sources that count one report half this book’s FLOPs everywhere), and whether a regularizer carries $\lambda$ or $\tfrac{\lambda}{2}$. Appendix A fixes the book’s conventions; check there before believing a source is wrong.

Real disagreements exist and this book names them. Chapter 3 argues directly against the capacity-control framing that Goodfellow presents as doctrine and Hastie et al. in its strongest classical form; read those as the position being attacked, not as background. Chapter 8 marks the tabular-data question contested, Chapter 5 the cost of non-deterministic reduction order. Each states which measurement would settle it, and the artifact runs whatever part of it fits on a CPU in thirty seconds.

Common Pitfall

Parallel reading degrades into serial skimming: you read three accounts of backpropagation, feel fluent, and cannot write the backward pass for a matmul from an empty file. Two sources plus one implementation beats five sources plus none, every time. Time-box the second source — it exists to unstick you on one step, not to be read cover to cover — and treat a chapter as unfinished until the artifact runs and you can explain the residual it prints.

E.7 The Gaps, Stated Plainly

Seven chapters have no adequate companion among the sources catalogued in Section E.2, and padding those rows would have been the easiest dishonesty in this book: Chapter 21, Chapter 37, and all five of Part VIII, with Chapters 28 and 36 close behind. Chapter 39 is the mildest case — its companion, Trefethen on approximation theory, exists but sits outside that list; the other six have nothing book-length at all. Likely The pattern is not random. A textbook enters this crosswalk when a topic has stabilized enough that someone would spend two years writing about it; the gaps are where that has not happened. Part VIII is worse than merely new — it sits between two literatures, and the cryptography texts covering CKKS correctly do not discuss transformers while the ML texts covering transformers do not discuss ciphertext.

The substitution protocol has three steps. First, read the primary paper in Appendix D for its assumptions rather than its results table — Section 40.5 demonstrates at length how much of a latency claim lives in its assumptions. Second, use an adjacent in-book chapter as the derivation source: Chapter 17 and Section 18.7 stand in for the background of Chapter 21; Chapters 33, 38 and 39 compose into Chapter 41; Chapter 5 is the numerical grounding under Chapter 34. Third, run the artifact and change a parameter the paper held fixed. A gap chapter’s artifact does double duty as the only cross-check you have, and Appendix C states exactly what each verifies itself against.

Common Pitfall

Substituting a survey for the missing textbook. Surveys are useful as taxonomies and dangerous as sources of numbers: they copy latency and accuracy figures across papers using different sequence lengths, security parameters and hardware, and provenance is lost by the second citation. Chapters 40 and 42 both say so outright. Use a survey to find which papers exist, take the number from the paper, and record the conditions alongside it — the discipline Artifact 40.1 enforces mechanically.

E.8 Using the Crosswalk

Three ways this table earns its keep. As a prerequisite check: if a chapter’s derivation column names a source you have never opened, that is a signal about the chapter, not the source. Chapter 7 is hard without Bishop on the dual; Chapter 32 is hard without having seen a profiler trace of the kind the Ultra-Scale Playbook reproduces. As triage when you are stuck: being stuck on a derivation and on an implementation are different failures with different remedies, and the two columns separate them — if you cannot see why the algebra works, go left; if the algebra is clear and your code prints the wrong number, go right and diff against the implementation named there. As a reading order: the crosswalk thins from dense in Part I to nothing by Part VIII, a fair description of how much scaffolding exists at each depth. Read Parts I–III with a companion open, Part IV with Prince and Raschka open, Parts V–VI with CS336 and the Ultra-Scale Playbook open, and Part VIII with the papers open and the artifacts running, because there is nothing else.

Two standing cautions. Volatile entries are tagged: course offerings change their assignments between years, the Ultra-Scale Playbook is revised continuously, and Géron’s 2025 PyTorch edition replaced the earlier TensorFlow and Keras editions, so an older copy on your shelf will not match the API discussion cited here. Verify Check any dated entry against Appendix F before citing it. And one ordering rule governs conflicts: where a named source and this book disagree on a fact, the source wins, and the response is to correct the book rather than reconcile the two in your head. Where they disagree on a measurement, run the artifact.

Appendix D

Sources and Further Reading

Where this book and a source disagree, the source wins. That convention is stated in the book’s own writing contract and it is not modesty. This book compresses: it takes a result that a paper established over twenty pages with ablations and error bars and states it in one sentence with a number attached. Compression loses the conditions under which the number holds. Every claim here that matters enough to act on — a scaling exponent, a latency, a depth budget, a security level — should be checked against the entry below that owns it before you build on it. This appendix is therefore evaluative rather than bibliographic: each entry says what the source is actually good for, and, where the book takes a different line, says so. Sections D.1 through D.7 are organized by what you are trying to learn; D.8 is about the harder problem of not falling behind. Volatile facts — editions, course offerings, preprint numbers that may acquire venues — carry a Verify tag.

D.1 Foundational Textbooks and Courses

Five books are the ones a reader is most likely to already own, and the ones whose attributions are easiest to get wrong; they are given here in full. Appendix E maps these and eight further sources chapter by chapter.

Aurélien Géron, Hands-On Machine Learning with Scikit-Learn and PyTorch: Concepts, Tools, and Techniques to Build Intelligent Systems. O’Reilly, 2 December 2025; ISBN 9798341607989. Verify This is the PyTorch edition, which replaces the earlier TensorFlow/Keras editions rather than supplementing them — if your copy says “Keras” on the cover, its second half no longer matches current practice. Best for: end-to-end project discipline, the scikit-learn API surface behind Part II, image pipelines and transfer learning for Chapter 20, and the practitioner’s account of automatic mixed precision that Chapter 5 derives from first principles. Where it differs from us: Géron treats the framework as the object of study; this book treats PyTorch as a cross-check against NumPy code you wrote yourself. Read him for the API, us for what the API is hiding.

Sebastian Raschka, Build a Large Language Model (From Scratch). Manning, October 2024; ISBN 9781633437166; code at github.com/rasbt/LLMs-from-scratch. The closest text in existence to Chapters 18, 19, 23, 26 and 33: tokenizer, dataloader, causal multi-head attention, KV cache, instruction templating with loss masking, and a generation loop, all written out line by line. Part VIII leans on it in a way that is easy to miss — Chapters 38 and 41 re-price exactly the plaintext block and the plaintext KV cache that Raschka builds, so having his code cold is a genuine prerequisite for the encrypted material, not a nicety.

Andrew W. Trask, Grokking Deep Learning. Manning, 2019. Backpropagation by hand, on scalars, before any framework exists. It is the right companion to Chapters 10 and 11 and the best available antidote to treating loss.backward() as magic. It stops well short of modern architectures; that is the correct scope for what it does.

Luis G. Serrano, Grokking Machine Learning. Manning, December 2021. The gentlest treatment of the classical algorithms that is still correct — no hand-waving that would have to be unlearned. Use it as a ramp into Part II. Its deliberate avoidance of linear algebra means it cannot reach the SVD-basis view of ridge regression in Chapter 6 or the spectral account of PCA in Chapter 9; for those, go to ESL and Bishop.

Tanmoy Chakraborty, Introduction to Large Language Models (Wiley India, 2025) Verify and NPTEL course 106102576, “Introduction to Large Language Models (LLMs)”. The course is taught by Prof. Tanmoy Chakraborty (IIT Delhi) with Prof. Soumen Chakrabarti (IIT Bombay); lectures are free on YouTube and the syllabus is public at nptel.ac.in/courses/106102576. This is the NLP-side framing of everything in Part V: it comes at language models from problems in natural language processing rather than from optimization, which makes it the best available second angle on Chapters 17, 24, 26, 29 and 36. Its historical framing of the Kaplan-to-Chinchilla arc is more careful than most.

Attribution, Stated Once, Correctly

NPTEL 106102576 is Prof. Tanmoy Chakraborty’s course, co-taught with Prof. Soumen Chakrabarti. An earlier draft of this project attributed it to the wrong person, and misattributions of Indian academic courses propagate quickly through secondary listings because the same course runs under multiple SWAYAM offering codes each term. If you cite it, cite the NPTEL course page, not a course-aggregator card.

Beyond the crosswalk, six reference texts carry most of the load.

Goodfellow, Bengio & Courville, Deep Learning (MIT Press, 2016; free at deeplearningbook.org) remains canonical for Parts I–III: the linear-algebra and numerical-computation preliminaries behind Chapters 1 and 5, the information-theoretic derivation behind Chapter 2, BPTT and the vanishing-gradient analysis behind Chapter 17. Read its regularization chapter knowing that Chapter 14 disputes its organizing assumption — that capacity control is the binding constraint — for the large-data regime.

Simon J. D. Prince, Understanding Deep Learning (MIT Press, December 2023; ISBN 9780262048644; free PDF at udlbook.github.io). Verify The best diagrams in print, and this book carries none: Prince’s pictures of ReLU networks folding input space, of variance decay through depth, of momentum trajectories on ill-conditioned losses, and of the diffusion forward process are the visual complement to Chapters 10, 12, 13 and 35 respectively.

Kevin P. Murphy, Probabilistic Machine Learning: An Introduction (MIT Press, 2022) and Advanced Topics (MIT Press, 2023). Advanced Topics is the probabilistic lookup volume for this book: the measure-theoretic statements Chapter 2 makes informally, the variational machinery behind the ELBO in Chapter 9, the Gaussian-process side of Chapter 7, the policy-gradient derivations under Chapter 27, and the full SDE treatment of diffusion behind Chapter 35. It is a reference, not a read-through.

Bishop, Pattern Recognition and Machine Learning (Springer, 2006; a free PDF is distributed by the author Verify) and Hastie, Tibshirani & Friedman, The Elements of Statistical Learning, 2nd ed. (Springer, 2009; free PDF from Stanford). Part II canon. Bishop owns the probabilistic derivations — logistic regression via IRLS, kernels and the SVM dual, PCA and EM. ESL owns shrinkage, the lasso path, CART and cost-complexity pruning, and Friedman’s original gradient boosting. ESL’s bias–variance chapter is also the strongest statement of exactly the picture Chapter 3 attacks; read it first, then read Chapter 3 as a rebuttal.

Three non-book sources matter more than most books. Stanford CS336, “Language Modeling from Scratch” (Percy Liang and Tatsunori Hashimoto; Spring 2025 lectures on YouTube, materials at cs336.stanford.edu) Verify is the closest course to Parts V and VI, and it is the assignments rather than the lectures that do the work: a byte-level BPE tokenizer trained at scale, a rotary cache you then break at long context, a Triton FlashAttention exercise, and a distributed-training assignment. Andrej Karpathy, Neural Networks: Zero to Hero, with micrograd, nanoGPT and minbpe, is the from-scratch pedagogy standard; the “makemore” episodes plot per-layer activation and gradient histograms and re-derive the Kaiming gain on screen, which is Chapter 12’s artifact in interactive form, and nanoGPT is the standard substrate for replicating the interpretability experiments in Chapter 37. The Ultra-Scale Playbook: Training LLMs on GPU Clusters (Hugging Face / nanotron, 2025) is the practical companion to all of Part VI, worked on real clusters with profiler traces rather than on paper. D.2 Architectures

Vaswani et al., Attention Is All You Need (NeurIPS 2017), is short and still worth reading in the original, but be aware that Chapter 18 does not follow it: the paper puts LayerNorm after the residual add, and every model trained since roughly 2019 puts it before. Read the paper for the idea and Chapter 18.5 for the block that people actually build.

He et al., Deep Residual Learning for Image Recognition (CVPR 2016), is the single most consequential architecture paper for Part III, and its argument is about optimization, not representation. Hochreiter & Schmidhuber, Long Short-Term Memory (Neural Computation 9(8), 1997), remains the correct source for the gating argument in Chapter 17; the constant-error-carousel motivation is clearer in the original than in any retelling.

For position: Su et al., RoFormer: Enhanced Transformer with Rotary Position Embedding (arXiv 2104.09864; Neurocomputing, 2024), for the derivation Chapter 19.4 follows; Press, Smith & Lewis, Train Short, Test Long (ALiBi, ICLR 2022); and Peng et al., YaRN (ICLR 2024), for context extension. Chapter 19.6 takes a harder line than any of these on base-frequency changes: the papers report what worked, not the failure modes.

Vision and multimodal: Dosovitskiy et al., An Image Is Worth 16x16 Words (ViT, ICLR 2021); Touvron et al., DeiT (ICML 2021); Radford et al., Learning Transferable Visual Models from Natural Language Supervision (CLIP, ICML 2021); and Zhai et al., Sigmoid Loss for Language Image Pre-Training (SigLIP, ICCV 2023, pp. 11975–11986). Those four are the ones Chapter 20 singles out as unusually readable primary sources, and they are — this is a corner of the literature where the papers beat the surveys. Liu et al., Swin Transformer (ICCV 2021), is the separate source for the hierarchical, windowed design of Section 20.7.

State space models: Gu, Goel & Ré, Efficiently Modeling Long Sequences with Structured State Spaces (S4, ICLR 2022); Gu & Dao, Mamba: Linear-Time Sequence Modeling with Selective State Spaces (COLM 2024); Dao & Gu, Transformers Are SSMs: Generalized Models and Efficient Algorithms Through Structured State Space Duality (ICML 2024). Chapter 21 says flatly that the primary papers are the only complete treatment, and adds Albert Gu’s Goomba Lab blog series as the clearest exposition of the semiseparable-matrix argument. This is also the corner of Part IV that Chapter 42.3 identifies as the most under-exploited opportunity under encryption.

Conditional computation: Shazeer et al., Outrageously Large Neural Networks (ICLR 2017), and Fedus, Zoph & Shazeer, Switch Transformers (JMLR 2022). Neither anticipates the shared-expert and fine-grained-expert designs of Chapter 22.5; for those the current model reports are the sources.

D.3 Training, Scaling, and Post-Training

Optimization: Kingma & Ba, Adam (ICLR 2015), and Loshchilov & Hutter, Decoupled Weight Decay Regularization (ICLR 2019), which is the AdamW correction Chapter 13.4 derives. For the matrix-preconditioning line ending in Chapter 13.6, Liu et al., Muon Is Scalable for LLM Training (arXiv 2502.16982, 2025), is the first paper to report Muon at a scale where the claim can be assessed; Keller Jordan’s original blog post is the clearer derivation and the paper is the better evidence. Verify

Hyperparameter transfer: Yang et al., Tensor Programs V: Tuning Large Neural Networks via Zero-Shot Hyperparameter Transfer (arXiv 2203.03466; NeurIPS 2021), is the muP source for Chapter 15. Read it for the coordinate-check methodology in particular — Section 15.4 exists because the parametrization is easy to get subtly wrong and the check is the only thing that catches it. McCandlish et al., An Empirical Model of Large-Batch Training (arXiv 1812.06162), owns the gradient-noise-scale argument behind Chapter 15.7.

Scaling laws: Kaplan et al., Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models (arXiv 2001.08361, 2020), and Hoffmann et al., Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models (Chinchilla, NeurIPS 2022). Chapter 24 treats these as a pair whose disagreement is the point, and it insists on two follow-ups that most secondary accounts omit: Besiroglu et al.’s replication of the Chinchilla fit (arXiv 2404.10102), which corrects the published parametric estimates, and Muennighoff et al., Scaling Data-Constrained Language Models (arXiv 2305.16264; NeurIPS 2023), which is the source for the repeated-data regime in Chapter 24.6. If you quote a Chinchilla exponent, quote it from the replication.

Tokenization: Sennrich, Haddow & Birch, Neural Machine Translation of Rare Words with Subword Units (ACL 2016), for BPE, and Kudo, Subword Regularization (ACL 2018), for the unigram model. Both predate the pathologies catalogued in Chapter 23.6, which have no canonical paper and are documented mostly in issue trackers.

Post-training: Ouyang et al., Training Language Models to Follow Instructions with Human Feedback (InstructGPT, NeurIPS 2022), for the three-stage recipe; Rafailov et al., Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model Is Secretly a Reward Model (NeurIPS 2023), for the derivation Chapter 27.5 reproduces and for the assumptions it makes silently; Shao et al., DeepSeekMath (arXiv 2402.03300, 2024), for GRPO. Parameter-efficient adaptation: Hu et al., LoRA (ICLR 2022), and Dettmers et al., QLoRA (NeurIPS 2023), the latter also being the reference for NF4 and paged optimizers in Chapter 26.5.

D.4 Systems and Efficiency

The FlashAttention line is short and readable end to end: Dao, Fu, Ermon, Rudra & Ré (NeurIPS 2022); Dao, FlashAttention-2 (ICLR 2024); Shah et al., FlashAttention-3 (NeurIPS 2024). Read them in order — each one changes what the bottleneck is, and reading only the third leaves you unable to say why. Milakov & Gimelshein, Online Normalizer Calculation for Softmax (arXiv 1805.02867, 2018), is the short prerequisite for all three and is the actual content of Chapter 31.2.

Serving: Kwon et al., Efficient Memory Management for Large Language Model Serving with PagedAttention (SOSP 2023), is the vLLM paper and the source for Chapter 33.3; Leviathan, Kalman & Matias, Fast Inference from Transformers via Speculative Decoding (ICML 2023), for Chapter 33.5, where the acceptance-rate arithmetic is what matters and the wall-clock numbers are hardware-bound.

Distributed training: Rajbhandari et al., ZeRO (SC 2020); Shoeybi et al., Megatron-LM (arXiv 1909.08053); Huang et al., GPipe (NeurIPS 2019). These three define the vocabulary of Chapter 32, but none of them composes the parallelisms the way current practice does; the Ultra-Scale Playbook is better on composition and on the failure modes of Chapter 32.9.

Quantization: Dettmers et al., LLM.int8() (NeurIPS 2022), for outlier features; Frantar et al., GPTQ (ICLR 2023); Lin et al., AWQ (MLSys 2024). Quantization is the one topic in Part VI whose numbers move fastest, and it is also where the plaintext and encrypted halves of the book meet: Chapter 34 and Chapter 39 are solving the same problem — how much precision can you give up before the model changes behavior — against completely different cost models.

D.5 Generative Modeling

Kingma & Welling, Auto-Encoding Variational Bayes (ICLR 2014), is where Chapter 35.1 starts. Ho, Jain & Abbeel, Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (NeurIPS 2020), is the paper that made the discrete formulation work; Song et al., Score-Based Generative Modeling Through Stochastic Differential Equations (ICLR 2021), is the continuous-time unification that Chapter 35.3 and 35.4 follow, and it is the one to read if you only read one. D.6 Evaluation, Interpretability, and Safety

Chapter 37 says plainly that no textbook in the book’s list covers interpretability, and that remains true. The field publishes on transformer-circuits.pub, on arXiv, and on the Alignment Forum, in that order of density. The four papers Chapter 37 is built on are Elhage et al., Toy Models of Superposition (2022), which is the source of the phase-transition picture in Section 37.2; Olsson et al., In-Context Learning and Induction Heads (2022); Bricken et al., Towards Monosemanticity (2023); and Templeton et al., Scaling Monosemanticity (2024). All four are web-native publications with interactive figures, and the figures carry argument that the text does not — do not read them as PDFs.

Evaluation has one genuinely contested pair, and Chapter 29.5 presents both sides: Wei et al., Emergent Abilities of Large Language Models (TMLR 2022), against Schaeffer, Miranda & Koyejo, Are Emergent Abilities of Large Language Models a Mirage? (NeurIPS 2023). The dispute is about whether discontinuity is a property of the model or of the metric, and it is settled empirically by re-scoring the same checkpoints under a continuous metric — which is what the second paper does and what you should do before repeating either claim. Contested

For benchmark infrastructure: Liang et al., Holistic Evaluation of Language Models (HELM, TMLR 2023), for the multi-metric framing; Hendrycks et al., Measuring Massive Multitask Language Understanding (MMLU, ICLR 2021), still the most-cited and most-contaminated benchmark in the field; Chiang et al., Chatbot Arena (ICML 2024), for the pairwise-ranking methodology and its documented biases. EleutherAI’s lm-evaluation-harness is the reference implementation of the scoring protocols in Chapter 29.2, and reading its code is the fastest way to understand why the same benchmark yields different numbers in different papers.

D.7 Encrypted Machine Learning

Part VIII is the half of this book with no textbook. Everything below is a primary source, and the field’s norms make that unusually workable: cryptography publishes preprints on the IACR Cryptology ePrint Archive with stable identifiers, and most of these papers ship artifacts.

The scheme. Cheon, Kim, Kim & Song, Homomorphic Encryption for Arithmetic of Approximate Numbers (ASIACRYPT 2017, pp. 409–437), is CKKS, and Chapter 38 deliberately does not re-derive it. Cheon, Han, Kim, Kim & Song, Bootstrapping for Approximate Homomorphic Encryption (EUROCRYPT 2018; ePrint 2018/153), is the refresh procedure whose cost dominates every budget in Chapters 38.6 and 41.4.

Parameters and security. Chapter 38 cites the Homomorphic Encryption Standard v1.1 (homomorphicencryption.org, November 2018) for parameter tables. Use it, but know that it has been effectively superseded: Bossuat et al., Security Guidelines for Implementing Homomorphic Encryption (IACR Communications in Cryptology 1(4), January 2025; ePrint 2024/463), carries updated security tables and functional parameter sets for all common schemes, and is the document to check a concrete parameter choice against today. Verify Li & Micciancio, On the Security of Homomorphic Encryption on Approximate Numbers (EUROCRYPT 2021; ePrint 2020/1533), is the paper that showed plain IND-CPA is insufficient for approximate schemes; Chapter 42 treats the resulting IND-CPA-D notion as a live constraint on deployment, not a footnote.

Approximation. Trefethen, Approximation Theory and Approximation Practice, Extended Edition (SIAM; ISBN 9781611975932) Verify is the source for Chebyshev interpolation, equioscillation and Remez done properly, and it is a genuinely enjoyable book. For comparison and sign specifically — the machinery every CKKS system now ships and which Chapter 39.5 builds — the two sources are Cheon, Kim, Kim, Lee & Lee, Numerical Method for Comparison on Homomorphically Encrypted Numbers (ASIACRYPT 2019, pp. 415–445; ePrint 2019/417), and Cheon, Kim & Kim, Efficient Homomorphic Comparison Methods with Optimal Complexity (ASIACRYPT 2020, LNCS 12492, pp. 221–256). Both are short.

Systems. Chapter 40 splits these into an interactive and a non-interactive line, and the split is the right way to read them.

Surveys for Taxonomy, Never for Numbers

Chapters 40 and 42 both state this rule and it is the single most useful habit in this literature. A survey’s comparison table flattens away the model, the sequence length, the threat model, the hardware, and whether the reported latency includes setup and key generation. Every one of those changes the number by an order of magnitude. Take the taxonomy from the survey; take the number from the paper, and take it with the configuration attached.

Libraries. OpenFHE is the most complete and the best documented, and its documentation is a legitimate source for what a production library exposes. Microsoft SEAL, Lattigo (Go), HEaaN, and Zama’s Concrete/Concrete-ML round out the set; TenSEAL is the easiest entry point for a first encrypted tensor. Library APIs move faster than papers — check any call signature against the version you have installed rather than against a tutorial.

D.8 How to Keep Current

The half-life of Parts V through VIII is roughly eighteen months. Likely Parts I through III will not move in your lifetime. Budget your reading accordingly: the foundations reward depth once, the frontier rewards a cheap recurring habit.

The habit that works is venue-based rather than feed-based, because feeds select for novelty and venues select for having survived review.

Four practices are worth more than any reading list. Read the artifact before the abstract. If a paper ships code, the code answers questions the paper does not: what the baseline actually was, which numbers are measured and which are extrapolated, and what the configuration was. Chapter 40.5 is an entire section on reading a latency claim, and its method is to look for the configuration, not the headline. Reproduce one number. Not the whole paper — one number, on the smallest configuration the code supports. The gap between what you get and what is reported is the paper’s real error bar. Track the correction, not just the claim. Chinchilla’s published fit was corrected by a replication; emergence was reframed by a rebuttal; the 2018 HE parameter tables were superseded by the 2025 security guidelines. In each case the correction is less cited than the original and more correct. Prefer the source that states its own limits. The SoK written by library maintainers is more honest about parameter constraints than the survey written from abstracts, for the same reason a paper with an artifact is more honest than one without: someone had to make it run.

Finally, on this book’s own numbers. Every claim tagged Verify is a claim that was correct when measured and is expected to drift — hardware bandwidths, model versions, prices, library APIs, preprint venues. Appendix F collects the dated reference numbers in one place precisely so that they can be re-measured rather than trusted. When one of them disagrees with a source you have checked, the source wins, and the book is what needs updating.

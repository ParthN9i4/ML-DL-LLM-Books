Chapter 38 established what breaks when a transformer meets ciphertext; Chapter 39 built the polynomial machinery that repairs the non-polynomial core. This chapter reads the systems literature that assembles those pieces into end-to-end private inference — and its central claim is methodological: the published systems are mutually incomparable answers to different questions. An interactive 2PC system, a non-interactive FHE system, and a TEE hybrid differ in threat model, in who computes, in what travels over the network, and in what their evaluation sections chose to time. Reading this literature well is an extraction exercise — threat model, depth budget, packing, exclusions — performed before believing any latency number. We trace the interactive line (Iron to Nimbus), the non-interactive line (NEXUS to Powerformer), automation and hybrids (ATLAS, Bifrost), then close with what is actually deployed. Chapter 41 takes up serving at scale; Chapter 42 maps where a contribution fits.

40.1 The Taxonomy: FHE, 2PC, Hybrid

One paragraph of CKKS facts, used throughout and not re-derived (Chapter 38 has the details; for the cryptography itself see a dedicated FHE text such as the OpenFHE documentation's references). A CKKS ciphertext holds a vector of complex slots numbering half the ring dimension, which is typically $2^{15}$ or $2^{16}$ (the book's $N$ remains parameter count; ring dimension is written out); plaintext-ciphertext and ciphertext-ciphertext multiplications consume levels from a finite budget, with a rescaling after each; moving data between slots costs rotations (key switchings, each roughly as expensive as a multiplication); and when the level budget is exhausted, bootstrapping refreshes it at a cost of tens of levels' worth of work and depth. Every design decision in this chapter is an arrangement of these five facts.

Definition 40.1 (Protocol families for private transformer inference)

A 2PC-with-HE system splits the computation between client and server holding additive secret shares; linear layers use RLWE homomorphic encryption as a subprotocol, non-linear layers use secret sharing and oblivious transfer, and the client participates in rounds of interaction throughout inference. A non-interactive FHE system has the client send one encrypted input and receive one encrypted output; the server evaluates the entire network homomorphically, paying multiplicative depth (and usually bootstrapping) instead of communication. A TEE hybrid places some of the computation in attested trusted hardware, shifting part of the trust from cryptographic assumptions to a hardware vendor. A complete threat-model statement must specify: who is trusted (semi-honest vs. malicious parties, hardware vendors), what is hidden (inputs, outputs, weights, architecture, sequence length), and who must stay online.

The three families answer three different questions. 2PC answers: two mutually distrusting parties with a fast network want joint inference where the server's weights and the client's input both stay private — the client works and communicates throughout. Non-interactive FHE answers: a weak client wants to hand off encrypted input to a powerful server and disconnect — no per-layer rounds, but the server pays the full depth of the network in homomorphic work. TEE hybrids answer: you will trust Intel, AMD, NVIDIA, or Apple's silicon attestation, in exchange for near-plaintext speed on whatever runs inside the enclave. Certain None of these dominates another; they occupy different points in a space whose axes are trust, bandwidth, latency, and client effort.

40.1.1 The two currencies

The trade separating the first two families is concrete. In 2PC, each non-linear operator (every GELU, every softmax) triggers interaction: comparisons and piecewise evaluations become oblivious-transfer executions whose cost is measured in gigabytes and round trips. Multiplicative depth is nearly free — each multiplication is a cheap local operation on shares plus a small exchange — so 2PC systems approximate non-linearities with high-fidelity piecewise polynomials and pay in bandwidth. In non-interactive FHE the ledger inverts: bandwidth collapses to two ciphertext transfers, but every multiplication in every approximation consumes depth, and depth beyond the budget means bootstrapping. This is why Chapter 39's low-degree approximation results matter asymmetrically: shaving polynomial degree helps a 2PC system modestly and a FHE system enormously. Packing sits underneath both: interactive systems pack to minimize ciphertext traffic per matrix multiplication, while non-interactive systems pack to minimize rotations and levels per layer, and a packing optimal for one objective is generally not optimal for the other. 40.2 The Interactive Line: Iron to BOLT to BumbleBee to Nimbus

Iron (NeurIPS 2022) initiated serious study of transformer 2PC in the client-server setting. Its contributions set the template every successor follows: a customized HE matrix-multiplication protocol with a compact packing the paper credits with $\sqrt{m}\times$ less communication than prior work ($m$ the output rows), plus dedicated protocols for softmax, GELU, and LayerNorm that the paper reports cut roughly half the communication and computation of general-purpose protocols. The absolute costs were still enormous — hundreds of gigabytes per BERT-class inference — which defined the next three years' agenda: shrink linear-layer HE traffic and non-linear protocol traffic simultaneously, because Amdahl's law governs a pipeline where both halves are expensive. Certain

BOLT (IEEE S&P 2024, ePrint 2023/1893) attacked both halves and added a third front: machine-learning-side surgery. Its HE matrix multiplication uses tighter packing; its GELU and softmax use piecewise-polynomial protocols; and its "word elimination" prunes redundant tokens before they enter the expensive pipeline — an early instance of the now-standard observation that the cheapest ciphertext operation is the one you delete from the graph. The paper reports 10.91× communication reduction and 4.8–9.5× faster inference than the prior state of the art (Iron) across network settings. In absolute terms BOLT still moves tens of gigabytes per BERT-base inference at sequence length 128 — the NEXUS paper's cross-comparison places it near 61 GB, a number to attribute to NEXUS's measurement conventions, not BOLT's own tables. Verify

BumbleBee (NDSS 2025, ePrint 2023/1678, built on the SecretFlow SPU stack) is the first of the line to reach a 7B-parameter model. Its two contributions mirror the two halves: RLWE ciphertext compression for matrix multiplication that the paper reports cuts 80–90% of communication versus prior techniques, and activation protocols for GELU/SiLU and the exponentials inside softmax reported to cut 80–95% versus two prior methods. The headline: the paper reports generating one token of LLaMA-7B in about 8 minutes on CPUs (8-token prompt, per its Table IV), and claims 3× the speed of BOLT at one-tenth the communication. Note what the headline is: one generated token, an eight-token prompt, CPUs — a feasibility statement, not a serving benchmark. Certain

Nimbus (NeurIPS 2024, arXiv 2411.15707) shows the linear-layer half was still not done: it replaces the input-multiplied-by-weights view with an outer-product, row-wise encoding that removes expensive number-theoretic transforms and rotations from the online path, and pairs it with low-degree polynomial approximations of GELU and softmax fitted to the observed input distribution of those operators rather than to a worst-case interval — the frozen model is left untouched, and the paper reports an average accuracy loss of 0.08% against unprotected inference. The paper reports 2.9–12.5× on the linear layer, 2.9–4.0× on the polynomial approximations, and 2.7–4.7× end-to-end on BERT-base across LAN and WAN settings; LayerNorm it explicitly leaves unoptimized. The trend across the four systems is instructive: each generation's gains come roughly equally from better cryptographic packing and from making the workload more approximation-tolerant — here by exploiting distributional structure the plaintext model already has, which is the cheapest version of that move because it costs no retraining. Likely

40.3 The Non-Interactive Line: NEXUS, SHAFT, THOR, Powerformer

NEXUS (NDSS 2025, ePrint 2024/136) is the reference point for the pure-FHE answer: the client encrypts under RNS-CKKS, sends once, receives once, and can go offline. Everything in between — matrix multiplications against plaintext server-side weights, GELU, softmax, LayerNorm, even a final secure Argmax built from sign polynomials — happens on ciphertext, with bootstrapping (FHE-MP-CNN over SEAL; HEXL on Intel CPUs, Phantom on GPU) refreshing the level budget. Its enabling primitives are packing tricks: SIMD ciphertext compression/decompression and slot folding to keep the slot utilization high through shape-changing operations. Its parameter set makes the depth ledger explicit: ring dimension $2^{16}$ and a 1763-bit ciphertext modulus for 128-bit security give total multiplicative depth $L = 35$, of which bootstrapping reserves $K = 14$, leaving $L - K = 21$ levels for the network itself — a budget that must cover every matrix multiplication, every GELU polynomial, every LayerNorm inverse-square-root iteration between two bootstraps. Twenty-one levels for a twelve-layer encoder is why Chapter 39's degree accounting is not a stylistic preference. The paper reports 37.3 seconds for a BERT-based inference on GPU (a 42.3× speedup over its own CPU version) at 164 MB of bandwidth — which it reports as 372.5× less bandwidth than BOLT and 53.6× less than BumbleBee. Bandwidth is the point: two ciphertext transfers replace per-layer gigabytes, which is exactly what the weak-client threat model buys. Certain

SHAFT (NDSS 2025, ePrint 2025/2324, Distinguished Artifact Award) is deliberately placed in this section as a classification exercise: it ships in the same NDSS 2025 cohort, and it is not non-interactive. SHAFT is a secret-sharing 2PC framework integrated with CrypTen's PyTorch interface; its contribution is making the interaction constant-round rather than eliminating it — the paper reports the first constant-round softmax protocol, combining input clipping with an iteration derived from an ordinary-differential-equation characterization of the exponential, plus a Fourier-series GELU. Constant rounds attack WAN latency, where round trips, not bytes, dominate; the paper reports 4.6–5.3× speedups over BumbleBee on LANs and 2.9–4.4× on WANs, matching the FSS-based SIGMA's runtime with 25–41% less communication. If your database's consistency check flags SHAFT when someone records it as non-interactive, the check is doing its job. Certain

THOR (ACM CCS 2025, ePrint 2024/1881) stays fully non-interactive and pushes on the linear algebra: diagonal-major encodings and compact packing for plaintext-ciphertext matrix multiplication, and a baby-step–giant-step ciphertext-ciphertext multiplication for the attention score and value products, which are ciphertext-by-ciphertext because activations meet activations there (Chapter 38's observation that attention is the only place a served model multiplies two encrypted tensors). The paper reports kernel-level speedups of 5.3× over BOLT's plaintext-ciphertext product and 9.7× over Powerformer's ciphertext-ciphertext product, and end-to-end BERT-base inference at sequence length 128 in about 10 minutes on a single GPU. Softmax, LayerNorm, GELU, and tanh are handled by approximation plus adaptive iterative methods — Chapter 39's Newton/Goldschmidt machinery tuned per operator. Certain

Powerformer (ACL 2025, pp. 11090–11111; ePrint 2024/1429) represents the opposite philosophy within the same family: change the model, not just the evaluator. It distills BERT into an architecture where softmax is replaced by a "Batch Rectifier-Power max" — a power function requiring no exponential and no max — and LayerNorm is distilled into a linear function, leaving only GELU/tanh to approximate (by a pseudo-sign composite method). The paper reports a 45% computation-time reduction against the state-of-the-art HE-based private language model with no accuracy loss. The philosophical split — approximate the standard network faithfully (NEXUS, THOR) versus train an FHE-friendly network (Powerformer, and Chapter 39's polynomial-architecture line) — is the live axis of the non-interactive literature, and venue is a tell: the former publishes at security conferences, the latter at ACL and other ML venues. Likely

40.4 Automation and Hybrids: ATLAS and TEE–FHE Designs

Every system in Section 40.3 hand-tunes its approximations: iteration counts, polynomial degrees, clipping ranges, chosen once and applied uniformly to all layers. ATLAS (arXiv 2607.23478) automates this. It formulates per-layer approximation configuration as multi-objective optimization over latency and accuracy — 120 decision variables for BERT/ViT, 320 for LLaMA-3 8B by the paper's count — and searches under real constraints the paper is unusually candid about: each candidate takes 70–1,000 seconds to evaluate even in cleartext, and 35–50% of candidates are numerically invalid (a diverging Newton iteration anywhere kills the whole forward pass). The "one hour" of the title is the automated search budget replacing expert weeks; the payoff it reports is roughly 20% lower end-to-end inference latency than uniform configuration, which is the honest size of the prize. The interesting claim is the premise: layers tolerate different approximation error, so uniform configurations leave latency on the table — the same per-layer-sensitivity observation that drives mixed-precision quantization in Chapter 34. Likely

Bifrost (arXiv 2606.17421) is the hybrid answer, and its threat model deserves the careful read. Secrets are provisioned only to an attested CPU TEE; the GPU, its device memory, the driver/runtime stack, and the host software are all outside the trusted computing base. Linear layers (projections, FFN matrices) are delegated to the untrusted accelerator under CKKS; non-linear operators, attention-side control logic, and KV-state transitions execute in cleartext inside the TEE; and — the structurally important move — a decrypt-then-re-encrypt refresh inside the TEE replaces bootstrapping entirely. The TEE becomes a trusted bootstrapping oracle, deleting the single most expensive FHE operation at the price of hardware trust. The paper (Chen, Qin, Zhang, Zhang, and Gu, Shanghai Jiao Tong University) reports projected latency reductions of 9.25× on GPT-2 1.5B and 9.91× on LLaMA-3 8B in an estimator-style comparison that adopts the methodology of the Euston system, and for its Bifrost+ prefill/decode split, time-to-first-token reductions of 14.6–45.8× (GPT-2 124M) and 15.3–53.4× (Qwen3 0.6B) against direct CKKS deployments — "projected" meaning an estimator, not end-to-end wall clock, which is exactly the distinction Section 40.5 exists to teach. Verify

40.5 Reading a Latency Claim

A latency number from this literature is meaningful only as the tuple (threat model, model, sequence length, hardware, network emulation, exclusions, accuracy cost). The extraction checklist, in the order that catches the most errors: First, the threat model — a 2PC number includes network time under an emulated LAN/WAN and client compute; a FHE number includes neither, and a TEE number includes almost nothing cryptographic. Second, model and sequence length — attention cost under encryption grows quadratically in sequence length exactly as in Chapter 18, but rotation counts also grow with how sequence length interacts with slot packing, so a 128-token number does not extrapolate linearly. Third, hardware — CPU versus GPU is a 40× swing in NEXUS's own reporting. Fourth, exclusions: key generation, weight encoding or encryption, model loading, the amortization convention (per-input versus per-batch-of-slots), and whether the number is measured or projected. Fifth, the accuracy bill: a system that distilled the model (Powerformer) is not evaluating the same function as one that approximated it faithfully. The table below juxtaposes four headline numbers; per the rule this book applies everywhere, the adjacent sentence is part of the table: these numbers are not comparable with each other — they measure different models, sequence lengths, hardware, threat models, and exclusions, and no ranking may be read from the column. Example 40.1 (Cross-paper numbers do not reconcile, by arithmetic)

NEXUS reports 164 MB of bandwidth, described as 372.5× less than BOLT and 53.6× less than BumbleBee. Take the claims at face value and reconstruct: BOLT $\approx 0.164 \times 372.5 = 61.09$ GB; BumbleBee $\approx 0.164 \times 53.6 = 8.79$ GB. The implied BOLT-to-BumbleBee ratio is $372.5 / 53.6 = 6.95$. But BumbleBee's own abstract claims one-tenth of BOLT's communication — a ratio of 10. Both papers are reporting honestly; $6.95 \neq 10$ because the two comparisons were run at different configurations (models, token counts, protocol variants). The discrepancy factor $10/6.95 \approx 1.44$ is the measurement-convention noise floor of this literature: any cross-system difference smaller than roughly 1.5× is not evidence of anything. Likely

Common Pitfall

Building a comparison table by pasting each paper's headline number from its own abstract into a single latency column — the standard slide in this area — silently compares a 2PC LAN wall-clock (BumbleBee, CPUs, includes network) against a GPU FHE figure (NEXUS, excludes any client cost) against a cost-model projection (Bifrost, no wall clock at all). The concrete failure: a reader divides BumbleBee's 8 minutes by NEXUS's 37.3 seconds, concludes NEXUS is "12.9× faster than BumbleBee", and has compared a 7B-parameter decode step on CPUs to a BERT-scale encoder forward pass on a GPU — two numbers sharing no model, no hardware, no threat model, and no timing convention. Artifact 40.1 makes this bug structurally impossible: a latency field cannot exist without an exclusions field, and the printed table carries the incomparability warning in the output itself.

40.6 What Is Actually Deployed

Nothing from Sections 40.2–40.4 serves production LLM traffic. What is deployed, at fleet scale, is the trusted-hardware end of the spectrum: Apple's Private Cloud Compute (announced and deployed 2024) runs Apple Intelligence's server-side inference on custom Apple silicon with a hardened OS, cryptographic attestation against a public transparency log, and no persistent storage — client devices release request keys only to nodes whose attested software measurements match the log. At WWDC 2026 (8 June) Apple announced PCC expanding beyond its own data centers onto Google Cloud infrastructure, on a three-layer hardware trust stack — NVIDIA Blackwell GPUs in confidential-computing mode, Intel CPUs with TDX, and Google's Titan security chip — with the stateless-computation, no-privileged-access, non-targetability, and verifiable-transparency requirements held fixed. Verify The industry default for "private" hosted inference — confidential-computing GPU modes, enclave-based serving products — is likewise hardware trust, not cryptography.

Homomorphic encryption is in production — but for retrieval, not transformers. Apple ships BFV-based HE (its open-source Swift Homomorphic Encryption library) in Live Caller ID Lookup, which queries a server about a phone number without revealing it, and in Enhanced Visual Search in Photos, where the device sends an encrypted image embedding and the server computes the nearest matches homomorphically, with differential-privacy and anonymization layers on top. The deployed pattern is precise: HE for shallow, embedding-level computations with tiny depth budgets; TEEs for the transformer forward pass; FHE transformers nowhere. Certain Whether that gap closes is genuinely contested: one position holds that bootstrapping-inclusive costs (minutes per BERT-inference-equivalent on a GPU, per Section 40.3) sit 3–4 orders of magnitude from serving viability and hardware trust will keep winning; the other holds that the 2022–2026 trajectory — roughly $10^3\times$ aggregate improvement from packing, approximation, and GPU acceleration — plus FHE ASICs will make narrow high-value deployments (regulated-data inference) viable first. Contested The evidence that would settle it: a production FHE deployment of any transformer-class model with paying users, or five more years without one. This author's position: the hybrid designs of Section 40.4 ship first, because they buy the largest cost reduction for the smallest change in trust story — and Chapter 42 treats the remaining gaps as a research map.

Artifact 40.1 — A System-Comparison Database with Sourcing Discipline

The artifact is data-as-code: ten systems, each a frozen record with seventeen fields (protocol family, threat model, scheme, softmax/norm/activation strategies, benchmark model, sequence length, hardware, reported latency, exclusions, venue, provenance), every field either sourced from the named paper or the literal string "n/r". The hard assertions enforce reading discipline rather than numerics: no record may claim non-interactivity while charging per-protocol client rounds (the SHAFT trap); no latency claim may exist without an explicit exclusions note; every record must carry venue and provenance; and coverage must include BOLT, BumbleBee, NEXUS, and SHAFT. It then prints the strategy table grouped by family and a second table demonstrating why the latency column cannot be read as a ranking, closing with the Example 40.1 arithmetic.

Key Takeaways
  • The literature splits into three protocol families — 2PC with HE subprotocols, non-interactive FHE, and TEE hybrids — that answer different questions under different threat models; no family dominates, and cross-family comparison of headline numbers is a category error. Certain
  • Every system's innovation budget goes to the same three operators — softmax, normalization, activation — and the strategies form a spectrum from faithful protocol-level approximation (Iron, BOLT, NEXUS, THOR), through distribution-aware low-degree fitting that leaves the model frozen (Nimbus), to model surgery that trains the non-linearity away (Powerformer). Certain
  • Interactive designs pay in bandwidth and rounds to keep multiplicative depth nearly free; non-interactive designs pay in depth and bootstrapping to collapse communication to two ciphertexts — packing is optimized against opposite objectives in the two families. Certain
  • A latency claim is only meaningful as the tuple (threat model, model, sequence length, hardware, network emulation, exclusions, accuracy cost); cross-paper reconstructions disagree by ~1.5× even between honest papers, which is the measurement noise floor of the field. Likely
  • What is deployed today is trusted hardware for transformer inference (Apple's Private Cloud Compute) and homomorphic encryption only for shallow retrieval workloads (Live Caller ID Lookup, Enhanced Visual Search); no FHE transformer serves production traffic as of this writing. Verify

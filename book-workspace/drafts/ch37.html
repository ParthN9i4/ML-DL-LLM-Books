37.1 Why Neurons Are Polysemantic

Look at almost any single neuron in a trained language model and you find it fires on an incoherent grab-bag: one MLP unit responds to Korean text, DNA sequences, and legal boilerplate. This polysemanticity is not sloppy training; it is the optimal use of limited dimensions, and the argument is geometric. The working assumption of the field is the linear representation hypothesis: a model's "features" — interpretable properties of the input like is-Python-code or refers-to-the-Golden-Gate-Bridge — are encoded as directions in activation space, with the feature's strength as the coefficient. Likely There is strong evidence for many linearly represented features (Section 37.5 steers with them), and known counterexamples such as circular representations of days-of-week, so the hypothesis is a good default rather than a law. Likely

Definition 37.1 (Superposition hypothesis)

A layer with activation dimension $d$ represents $m \gg d$ features as directions $\{f_i\}_{i=1}^m \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ that are not mutually orthogonal, tolerating interference $\langle f_i, f_j\rangle \neq 0$ because features are sparse: few are active on any one input, so interference is rare and small. The network is then a lossy simulation of a much larger network with one dimension per feature (Elhage et al., Toy Models of Superposition, 2022). Certain (as a demonstrated phenomenon in toy models; its prevalence in frontier models is inferred).

Why can $d$ dimensions hold far more than $d$ almost-orthogonal directions? Exact orthogonality caps you at $d$, but relaxing to near-orthogonality changes the count from linear to exponential — the same concentration phenomenon behind the Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma.

Theorem 37.1 (Near-orthogonal packing)

For any $\epsilon \in (0,1)$ there exist $K \geq e^{d\epsilon^2/4}$ unit vectors in $\mathbb{R}^d$ with $|\langle f_i, f_j\rangle| \leq \epsilon$ for all $i \neq j$.

Proof sketch. Draw $K$ vectors uniformly from the sphere. For a fixed pair, the inner product concentrates: $\Pr[|\langle f_i,f_j\rangle| > \epsilon] \leq 2e^{-d\epsilon^2/2}$ (Lévy concentration of measure; the inner product with a fixed unit vector has variance $1/d$). A union bound over the $\binom{K}{2} < K^2/2$ pairs gives failure probability below $K^2 e^{-d\epsilon^2/2}$, which is under $1$ when $K \leq e^{d\epsilon^2/4}$, so a valid configuration exists. $\blacksquare$

The numbers are what matter. A random pair of unit vectors in $d = 4096$ (a mid-sized residual stream) has typical cosine $1/\sqrt{d} \approx 0.016$. At tolerance $\epsilon = 0.1$, Theorem 37.1 guarantees $e^{4096 \cdot 0.01/4} = e^{10.24} \approx 2.8 \times 10^4$ directions, and the bound is loose; tighter spherical-code bounds and empirical dictionary sizes suggest far more. This is why a neuron basis fails: features get distributed across near-orthogonal directions that have no reason to align with coordinate axes, so each axis (neuron) receives projections of many features — polysemanticity is the shadow of superposition on the standard basis. Certain

37.2 Superposition in a Toy Model

Elhage et al. (2022) made this trainable and quantitative with a model small enough to fully analyze, and Artifact 37.1 reproduces it. Data: $x \in \mathbb{R}^m$ with $m$ features, each independently active with probability $p$ (value uniform on $[0,1]$, else $0$), with importance weights $I_i = 0.9^i$. Model: compress to $d \ll m$ dimensions and reconstruct through a ReLU, $$\hat{x} = \mathrm{ReLU}(W^\top W x + b), \qquad W \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times m}, \qquad \mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}\Big[\textstyle\sum_i I_i (x_i - \hat{x}_i)^2\Big].$$ Column $W_i$ is the direction assigned to feature $i$; $\|W_i\| \approx 0$ means the feature is dropped. The result is a phase transition in $p$. Dense features ($p = 1$): the model keeps almost exactly the $d$ most important features orthogonally and drops the rest, like PCA. Sparse features ($p \ll 1$): it packs far more than $d$ features as non-orthogonal directions, in striking geometric arrangements (antipodal pairs, pentagons, tetrahedra) that trade interference against coverage. In our run ($d=5$, $m=20$), the dense model keeps exactly $5/20$ features while the sparse model at $p = 0.05$ keeps $15/20$ at relative loss $0.226$ — well below the $0.534$ floor that any $5$-feature model must pay in dropped importance mass. Certain (measured; Artifact 37.1).

Example 37.1 (Three features in two dimensions)

Why the ReLU is essential: place three unit feature directions at $120°$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$: $f_1 = (1,0)$, $f_2 = (-\tfrac12, \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2})$, $f_3 = (-\tfrac12, -\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2})$, so every pair has $\langle f_i, f_j\rangle = -\tfrac12$. Take $b = 0$. Encode $x = (1,0,0)$: $h = f_1$, and the reconstruction is $\hat{x}_i = \mathrm{ReLU}(\langle f_i, h\rangle)$, i.e. $\hat{x} = (\mathrm{ReLU}(1), \mathrm{ReLU}(-\tfrac12), \mathrm{ReLU}(-\tfrac12)) = (1,0,0)$ — exact, because the interference is negative and the ReLU clips it. Now activate two features, $x = (1,1,0)$: $h = f_1 + f_2 = (\tfrac12, \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2})$, giving $\hat{x}_1 = \mathrm{ReLU}(1 - \tfrac12) = \tfrac12$, $\hat{x}_2 = \mathrm{ReLU}(-\tfrac12 + 1) = \tfrac12$, $\hat{x}_3 = \mathrm{ReLU}(-\tfrac12 - \tfrac12) = 0$, so the squared error is $(\tfrac12)^2 + (\tfrac12)^2 = \tfrac12$. Superposition stores 3 features in 2 dimensions perfectly when one is active and pays $\tfrac12$ when two co-occur — a good trade exactly when co-occurrence is rare. That is the whole hypothesis in four lines of arithmetic.

37.3 Sparse Autoencoders

If activations $a \in \mathbb{R}^d$ are sparse combinations of directions from an unknown dictionary, recovering that dictionary is classical sparse coding (Olshausen & Field, 1996), scaled up. A sparse autoencoder learns an overcomplete dictionary $W_{\mathrm{dec}} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times d}$, $m \gg d$, and a sparse code $z \in \mathbb{R}^m_{\geq 0}$ per activation:

Definition 37.2 (Sparse autoencoder)

Encoder $z = \mathrm{ReLU}(W_{\mathrm{enc}}(a - b_{\mathrm{dec}}) + b_{\mathrm{enc}})$, decoder $\hat{a} = W_{\mathrm{dec}}^\top z + b_{\mathrm{dec}}$, trained on $$\mathcal{L} = \|a - \hat{a}\|_2^2 + \lambda \|z\|_1,$$ the $\ell_1$ term being the convex surrogate for the intractable $\|z\|_0$ (the same relaxation as the lasso, Chapter 14). Rows of $W_{\mathrm{dec}}$ are constrained to unit norm; the candidate "features" are those rows, and a latent's activations over a corpus are what you inspect to label it. TopK variants replace the $\ell_1$ term by keeping only the $k$ largest pre-activations per input (Gao et al., 2024), fixing $\|z\|_0 = k$ exactly. Certain

The $\ell_1$ penalty causes shrinkage: it pays $\lambda$ per unit of magnitude even on correctly-active latents, biasing $z$ low and reconstruction short. TopK, JumpReLU (Rajamanoharan et al., DeepMind 2024), and gated encoders exist mainly to remove this bias. Scaled up, the method produced the field's most visible results: Anthropic trained SAEs with up to 34M latents on Claude 3 Sonnet activations, finding features for sycophancy, security vulnerabilities, and the Golden Gate Bridge (Templeton et al., Scaling Monosemanticity, 2024), and OpenAI trained a 16M-latent TopK SAE on GPT-4 (Gao et al., 2024). Verify (latent counts; check the two papers).

Common Pitfall

Training an $\ell_1$ SAE without constraining decoder row norms. The penalty is on $\|z\|_1$ but the reconstruction only sees $W_{\mathrm{dec}}^\top z$, so gradient descent scales $z$ down by $c$ and $W_{\mathrm{dec}}$ up by $c$, driving the sparsity penalty toward zero without any actual sparsity — the loss curve looks great and the dictionary is garbage. Renormalize decoder rows every step (as Artifact 37.1 does) or project the gradient. A second real bug: dead latents. In large SAEs a substantial fraction of latents can stop firing entirely mid-training, silently shrinking your dictionary; production runs monitor firing frequency and resample or ghost-grad dead latents.

37.3.1 The Sparsity–Fidelity Frontier

Sweeping $\lambda$ (or $k$) traces a frontier between $L_0 = \|z\|_0$ and reconstruction error, usually reported as fraction of variance unexplained. You cannot read quality off either axis alone, and Artifact 37.1's frontier on planted data shows why. With true sparsity $3$: at $k=1$ the SAE is starved (normalized MSE $0.48$, recovers $21/32$ planted features); at $k=3$ it is exact (MSE $1.7\times 10^{-9}$, $32/32$ recovered); at $k=6$ and $k=12$ reconstruction stays essentially perfect ($10^{-8}$–$10^{-7}$) while recovery collapses to $24/32$ and then $2/32$. An over-budgeted SAE reconstructs perfectly using a wrong dictionary — distributing each true feature across several latents. On real models there is no planted ground truth, so a low-MSE point on the frontier is not evidence the dictionary is right; mis-set sparsity provably yields confidently wrong features (Chanin et al., Sparse but Wrong, 2025). Certain (the measured frontier); Likely (the transfer of this diagnosis to LLM SAEs).

37.3.2 Failure Modes

Beyond shrinkage and dead latents, two failure modes are structural. Feature splitting: as $m$ grows, a "math" latent splits into "algebra" and "geometry" latents, with no principled level at which to stop — the dictionary is resolution-dependent, which undermines the claim that latents are the model's units. Feature absorption (Chanin et al., 2024, arXiv:2409.14507): with hierarchical features (starts-with-S $\supset$ short), sparsity is minimized by having the specific latent absorb the general one, so the "starts with S" latent mysteriously fails to fire on "short" — a gerrymandered latent that looks monosemantic on top activations and lies about its own extension. Varying size or sparsity does not fix it. Certain (both are empirically documented across hundreds of SAEs).

Then came the walk-backs. In March 2025 Google DeepMind's mechanistic interpretability team published negative results — on downstream tasks such as concept detection and steering-based control, SAEs failed to beat simple baselines like linear probes — and announced it was deprioritizing SAE research. Certain (the announcement is public). Follow-up work converged on a narrower positive claim: SAEs are useful for discovering unknown concepts (hypothesis generation, unsupervised audits), not for acting on known concepts, where a supervised probe trained on labels is cheaper and better (arXiv:2506.23845, 2025). Likely The honest summary: SAEs demonstrably find human-interpretable directions, and it is not demonstrated that those directions are the units the model itself computes with — there is no ground truth to check against, which is precisely why Artifact 37.1 plants one. Contested — optimists read absorption and splitting as engineering problems (fixable with better priors), skeptics as evidence the "true features" framing is wrong. A decisive test would be an SAE-derived circuit that predicts model behavior out of distribution better than behavioral baselines; nothing published clears that bar as of this writing.

37.4 Circuits and Patching

Features are nouns; circuits are the verbs connecting them. Three tools, in increasing causal strength. The logit lens (nostalgebraist, 2020) applies the final unembedding to intermediate residual streams, reading each layer's "current best guess" of the output; it shows predictions refining layer by layer but is purely correlational and fails on models whose intermediate bases drift (the tuned lens of Belrose et al., 2023, patches this with per-layer affine maps). Activation patching is the causal upgrade: run the model on a clean prompt and a corrupted prompt, splice one activation from the clean run into the corrupted run, and measure how much of the correct behavior is restored. Components whose patching restores behavior are causally implicated; this is causal mediation analysis (Vig et al., 2020) and the "causal tracing" behind the ROME editing work (Meng et al., 2022). It localizes computation without explaining it, and results are sensitive to the choice of corruption and metric — a documented source of interpretability illusions. Certain

The canonical discovered circuit is the induction head (Olsson et al., 2022). On a sequence containing $\ldots A\,B \ldots A$, an induction circuit predicts $B$: a first-layer "previous-token" head writes each token's predecessor into its residual position; a second-layer head then queries for positions whose previous token matches the current token and copies what followed. Two heads, composing across layers, implement "find the last time this happened and repeat what came next" — a primitive of in-context learning. The circuit appears abruptly during training as a visible loss-curve bump, and its formation co-occurs with the jump in in-context learning ability; in attention-only 2-layer models the mechanism is fully verified, while in large models the same co-occurrence is strong but the causal story is coarser. Certain (small models) / Likely (large ones).

The current frontier is attribution graphs (Anthropic, March 2025: Circuit Tracing and On the Biology of a Large Language Model). Cross-layer transcoders — SAE-like sparse replacements trained to imitate the MLPs — make the whole forward pass approximately a sparse linear graph from input tokens through features to logits, for one prompt at a time. On Claude 3.5 Haiku this surfaced multi-step internal reasoning ("Dallas" $\to$ Texas $\to$ "Austin"), forward planning of rhyme words in poetry before the line is written, and cases where the model's chain-of-thought explanation provably does not match its internal computation — motivated reasoning, mechanistically caught. Certain (the papers' claims about studied prompts). The method's own authors state the limits: the transcoder is an approximation with unexplained error terms, graphs are per-prompt, and a minority-to-moderate fraction of attempted prompts yield satisfying graphs, each requiring hours of human analysis. Verify (coverage fraction; check the Methods paper's limitations section).

37.5 Activation Steering

Everything above tries to explain; steering just intervenes. Add a vector to the residual stream at inference — $a \leftarrow a + \alpha v$ — where $v$ is a difference of activation means between contrasting prompts (Turner et al., Activation Addition, 2023; Zou et al., Representation Engineering, 2023) or an SAE decoder row clamped on (Templeton et al., 2024 — the "Golden Gate Claude" demo, where one clamped feature made the model identify as the bridge). The sharpest result is refusal: across 13 open models, refusal behavior is mediated by a single direction — ablate it and the model stops refusing harmful requests; add it and the model refuses harmless ones (Arditi et al., 2024). Certain That is simultaneously an interpretability success, a jailbreak (weight-level, on open models), and the cleanest statement of the field's condition: we can find a one-dimensional causal handle on a safety-critical behavior without anything like a mechanistic account of how refusal is computed. Steering validates the linear-representation hypothesis feature-by-feature; it does not require, and has not yet delivered, understanding. Artifact 37.1 runs the honest version of this experiment — clamping a recovered latent moves the reconstruction along the planted ground-truth direction with cosine $+1.0000$ while a control feature moves by only $+0.11$ — which is exactly the standard of evidence (targeted effect, null control) a steering claim needs. Certain (measured).

37.7 Open Problems

Sharkey et al.'s survey (Open Problems in Mechanistic Interpretability, 2025, arXiv:2501.16496) is the field's own list; the load-bearing items are these. No ground truth. Every decomposition method is validated by proxy metrics (sparsity, fidelity, auto-interp scores) because the "true features" of a real model are unobservable; Artifact 37.1's planted-dictionary test is the standard trick, and it only certifies the method on data that satisfies the method's assumptions. Are features even the right ontology? Splitting and absorption suggest the model may not have a discrete dictionary to find; computation may live in overlapping subspaces or in the attention pattern itself (attention-head superposition), which SAEs on residual activations cannot see. Scalability of circuits. An attribution graph explains one prompt after hours of expert effort; a safety case needs quantified coverage of a behavior class over all prompts. Nothing bridges that gap yet. Validation asymmetry. Steering proves a direction is causally sufficient to move behavior, not that your story about it is right — the refusal direction moves refusal while the mechanism remains unknown. Usefulness. The sharpest challenge from the 2025 negative results: for nearly every downstream task with labels, a linear probe beats the interpretable pipeline. Interpretability's comparative advantage must be the label-free regime — auditing for behaviors you did not anticipate — and demonstrating a decisive win there is open. Likely (this framing of the frontier).

37.8 How to Read This Field

Interpretability publishes fast, mostly outside peer review — transformer-circuits.pub, arXiv, the Alignment Forum — and its half-life is short: within roughly eighteen months, SAEs went from "biggest breakthrough in the field" to a major lab publicly deprioritizing them, which is a sign of health (the walk-back came from measurement) but demands reading habits. First, separate the observation from the framing: "we found a latent that fires on deception" is an observation; "the model has a deception feature" is a theory, and absorption shows how the first can hold while the second fails. Second, demand the causal test and the control: a steering result without a null direction, or a patching result under one corruption scheme, is an anecdote — interpretability illusions with exactly this shape are documented. Third, weight negative results and limitation sections heavily; this field's best papers (Toy Models, the Biology paper) devote unusual space to their own failure modes, and authors who don't are behind the field's own standard. Fourth, keep the safety ledger honest in both directions: the empirical rows of Section 37.6 are real, replicated, and disturbing, and no published result shows unprompted scheming in deployment. A reader who rounds either half off is not reading the evidence. The practical bar for any claim you plan to build on is the one this chapter's artifact enforces: known ground truth in, stated recovery threshold, causal intervention with a control, numbers printed. Certain (as method; the field citations above are dated and moving Verify).

Artifact 37.1 — Superposition, a TopK SAE, and a Causal Steering Test

Pure-NumPy implementation of the chapter's three load-bearing experiments, with hard assertions. (1) The Elhage toy model ($m{=}20$ features, $d{=}5$ dims) with manual backprop through $\mathrm{ReLU}(W^\top W x + b)$: asserts the sparse regime packs $\geq 2d$ features below the provable $d$-feature loss floor while the dense regime keeps $\leq d{+}1$. (2) A TopK SAE trained on activations synthesized from a planted dictionary of 32 near-orthogonal directions in $\mathbb{R}^{64}$ (3 active per sample): asserts $\geq 80\%$ of planted features are recovered at max-cosine $> 0.9$, and prints the $L_0$-versus-error frontier. (3) Clamping the recovered latent for a planted feature on inputs where that feature is inactive: asserts the reconstruction shift aligns with the planted direction and not with a control feature. The manual TopK gradient is verified against torch autograd in float64 when torch is available.

"""Artifact 37.1 -- Superposition, sparse autoencoders, and a causal steering test.

Three experiments, pure NumPy (torch appears only as an autograd cross-check):
  (1) The toy-superposition model of Elhage et al. (2022): an autoencoder with
      m=20 features and d=5 dimensions. With SPARSE features it packs >2d
      features at low loss (superposition); with DENSE features it keeps ~d.
  (2) A TopK sparse autoencoder (Gao et al., 2024) trained on activations
      built from a KNOWN planted dictionary; we assert recovery of >=80% of
      planted features at cosine similarity > 0.9.
  (3) A causal steering test: clamping a recovered latent moves the
      reconstruction along the planted direction; a control feature does not.
Also prints the L0-vs-reconstruction-error frontier (the sparsity-fidelity
trade-off of Section 37.3.1). Runs CPU-only in ~15 s.
"""
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# ----------------------------------------------------------- tiny Adam in NumPy
def adam(params, grads, state, lr, t, b1=0.9, b2=0.999, eps=1e-8):
    for i, (p, g) in enumerate(zip(params, grads)):
        m, v = state[i]
        m[:] = b1 * m + (1 - b1) * g
        v[:] = b2 * v + (1 - b2) * g * g
        p -= lr * (m / (1 - b1**t)) / (np.sqrt(v / (1 - b2**t)) + eps)

# ============ (1) TOY MODEL OF SUPERPOSITION: x_hat = ReLU(W^T W x + b) =======
def train_toy(p_active, d=5, m=20, steps=6000, batch=512, lr=3e-3, seed=1):
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    W = r.normal(0, 0.3, (d, m)); b = np.zeros(m)
    I = 0.9 ** np.arange(m)                       # importance weights
    st = [(np.zeros_like(W), np.zeros_like(W)), (np.zeros_like(b), np.zeros_like(b))]
    for t in range(1, steps + 1):
        X = (r.random((batch, m)) < p_active) * r.random((batch, m))
        H = X @ W.T                               # (B,d)  compress
        Z = H @ W + b                             # (B,m)  reconstruct
        Xh = np.maximum(Z, 0)
        dXh = 2 * I * (Xh - X) / batch            # grad of mean_B sum_i I_i e_i^2
        dZ = dXh * (Z > 0)
        dW = H.T @ dZ + (dZ @ W.T).T @ X          # two paths: Z=HW and H=XW^T
        adam([W, b], [dW, dZ.sum(0)], st, lr, t)
    # importance-weighted eval loss, relative to the zero-predictor
    X = (r.random((8192, m)) < p_active) * r.random((8192, m))
    Xh = np.maximum(X @ W.T @ W + b, 0)
    rel = (I * (Xh - X)**2).sum() / (I * X**2).sum()
    return W, rel

# ================== (2) TopK SPARSE AUTOENCODER, manual backprop ==============
def gen_acts(r, B, F, k_true=3, exclude=None):
    """Activations a = sum_i z_i f_i with exactly k_true planted features on."""
    n = F.shape[0]
    scores = r.random((B, n))
    if exclude is not None:
        scores[:, exclude] = -1.0                 # never activate this feature
    idx = np.argpartition(scores, -k_true, 1)[:, -k_true:]
    Zt = np.zeros((B, n))
    np.put_along_axis(Zt, idx, r.uniform(0.5, 1.5, (B, k_true)), 1)
    return Zt @ F

def sae_forward(A, We, be, Wd, bd, k):
    Ac = A - bd
    P = Ac @ We.T + be                            # (B,m) pre-codes
    idx = np.argpartition(P, -k, 1)[:, -k:]
    mask = np.zeros_like(P); np.put_along_axis(mask, idx, 1.0, 1)
    Z = P * mask                                  # TopK latents
    return Ac, P, mask, Z, Z @ Wd + bd

def sae_grads(A, We, be, Wd, bd, k):
    B = A.shape[0]
    Ac, P, mask, Z, Ah = sae_forward(A, We, be, Wd, bd, k)
    R = Ah - A
    dAh = 2 * R / B                               # loss = mean_B ||a_hat - a||^2
    dWd, dbd = Z.T @ dAh, dAh.sum(0)
    dP = (dAh @ Wd.T) * mask                      # TopK: grad only through kept
    dWe, dbe = dP.T @ Ac, dP.sum(0)
    dbd -= (dP @ We).sum(0)                       # bd also enters via Ac = A-bd
    return [dWe, dbe, dWd, dbd], (R**2).sum(1).mean()

def train_sae(F, d, m, k, steps=2500, batch=256, lr=2e-3, seed=2):
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    Wd = r.normal(size=(m, d)); Wd /= np.linalg.norm(Wd, axis=1, keepdims=True)
    We, be, bd = Wd.copy(), np.zeros(m), np.zeros(d)
    ps = [We, be, Wd, bd]
    st = [(np.zeros_like(p), np.zeros_like(p)) for p in ps]
    for t in range(1, steps + 1):
        g, _ = sae_grads(gen_acts(r, batch, F), We, be, Wd, bd, k)
        adam(ps, g, st, lr, t)
        Wd /= np.linalg.norm(Wd, axis=1, keepdims=True)   # unit decoder rows
    A = gen_acts(r, 4096, F)
    *_, Ah = sae_forward(A, We, be, Wd, bd, k)
    nmse = ((Ah - A)**2).sum() / ((A - A.mean(0))**2).sum()
    return (We, be, Wd, bd), nmse

if __name__ == "__main__":
    # ---- (1) superposition: sparse packs > d features, dense keeps ~ d ------
    d, m = 5, 20
    Ws, rel_s = train_toy(p_active=0.05)          # sparse regime
    Wd_, rel_d = train_toy(p_active=1.00)         # dense regime
    nf = lambda W: int((np.linalg.norm(W, axis=0) > 0.5).sum())
    ns, nd = nf(Ws), nf(Wd_)
    # any model representing only d of the m features pays at least the
    # importance mass of the m-d dropped features (E[x_i^2] equal across i):
    I = 0.9 ** np.arange(m)
    bound_d_feats = I[d:].sum() / I.sum()
    print(f"[toy] sparse p=0.05: {ns}/{m} features in {d} dims, rel-loss {rel_s:.4f}")
    print(f"[toy]   (best possible d-feature model on sparse data: {bound_d_feats:.4f})")
    print(f"[toy] dense  p=1.00: {nd}/{m} features in {d} dims, rel-loss {rel_d:.4f}")
    assert ns >= 2 * d, f"superposition failed: only {ns} features packed"
    assert nd <= d + 1, f"dense model kept {nd} > d+1 features"
    assert rel_s < 0.6 * bound_d_feats, "superposition loss not below d-feature bound"

    # ---- (2) SAE recovery of planted features + L0/error frontier -----------
    D, n_true, m_sae = 64, 32, 64
    F = rng.normal(size=(n_true, D)); F /= np.linalg.norm(F, axis=1, keepdims=True)
    print("\n[frontier]  L0(k) | norm. recon MSE | planted features recovered")
    models = {}
    for k in (1, 2, 3, 6, 12):
        params, nmse = train_sae(F, D, m_sae, k)
        C = F @ params[2].T                       # cosines: planted x decoder
        rec = int((C.max(1) > 0.9).sum())
        models[k] = (params, C)
        print(f"            {k:5d} | {nmse:15.2e} | {rec}/{n_true}")
    (We, be, Wd, bd), C = models[3]               # k = true sparsity
    frac = (C.max(1) > 0.9).mean()
    print(f"[sae] k=3 recovery: {frac:.0%} of planted features at cos > 0.9")
    assert frac >= 0.8, f"recovered only {frac:.0%} of planted features"

    # ---- (3) causal steering: clamp matched latent vs control ---------------
    tgt = int(C.max(1).argmax())                  # best-recovered planted feat
    lat = int(C[tgt].argmax())                    # its matching SAE latent
    ctrl = (tgt + 1) % n_true                     # a different planted feature
    A = gen_acts(rng, 512, F, exclude=tgt)        # target feature never active
    *_, Zb, Ab = sae_forward(A, We, be, Wd, bd, 3)
    Zc = Zb.copy(); Zc[:, lat] = 8.0              # clamp the latent on
    Delta = (Zc @ Wd + bd) - Ab                   # steering displacement
    cos = lambda u, v: float(u @ v / (np.linalg.norm(u) * np.linalg.norm(v)))
    c_t = cos(Delta.mean(0), F[tgt]); c_c = cos(Delta.mean(0), F[ctrl])
    print(f"[steer] cos(delta, planted target) = {c_t:+.4f}")
    print(f"[steer] cos(delta, control feat)   = {c_c:+.4f}")
    assert c_t > 0.9, "steering did not move along the planted direction"
    assert abs(c_c) < 0.35, "control direction moved too much"

    # ---- torch cross-check of the manual TopK-SAE gradient ------------------
    try:
        import torch
    except ImportError:
        torch = None
    if torch is None:
        print("[skipped: torch not installed]")
    else:
        r = np.random.default_rng(7); A0 = gen_acts(r, 64, F)
        g_np, _ = sae_grads(A0, We, be, Wd, bd, 3)
        tp = [torch.tensor(p, dtype=torch.float64, requires_grad=True)
              for p in (We, be, Wd, bd)]
        tA = torch.tensor(A0, dtype=torch.float64)
        Ac = tA - tp[3]; P = Ac @ tp[0].T + tp[1]
        v, i = torch.topk(P, 3, dim=1)
        Z = torch.zeros_like(P).scatter(1, i, v)
        ((Z @ tp[2] + tp[3] - tA)**2).sum(1).mean().backward()
        diff = max(float((t.grad - torch.tensor(g)).abs().max())
                   for t, g in zip(tp, g_np))
        print(f"[check] max |manual grad - torch autograd| = {diff:.2e}")
        assert diff < 1e-9, "manual gradient disagrees with autograd"
    print("\nAll assertions passed.")

Running it (15.5 s wall on CPU) prints the toy result first — sparse regime 15/20 features in 5 dims, rel-loss 0.2257 against the d-feature floor 0.5338, dense regime exactly 5/20 — then the frontier table quoted in Section 37.3.1 (normalized MSE 4.79e-01 at $k{=}1$ falling to 1.70e-09 at $k{=}3$, with recovery 21, 32, 32, 24, 2 out of 32 as $k$ sweeps 1, 2, 3, 6, 12), then k=3 recovery: 100% of planted features at cos > 0.9, the steering cosines +1.0000 (target) versus +0.1101 (control), an autograd agreement of 4.24e-21, and All assertions passed.

Key Takeaways
  • Neurons are polysemantic because concentration of measure lets $d$ dimensions hold exponentially many near-orthogonal directions, and sparse features make the resulting interference cheap; superposition is the trained-in exploitation of this, reproduced in Artifact 37.1 (15 features in 5 dimensions). Certain
  • Sparse autoencoders recover planted dictionaries essentially perfectly when their sparsity matches the data's, but the frontier is treacherous: at $k$ above the true sparsity, reconstruction stays perfect while the dictionary silently collapses (measured: $32/32 \to 2/32$ recovered at identical near-zero MSE). Certain
  • Feature splitting and absorption are structural failures of sparsity-optimized dictionaries, and 2025's negative results (SAEs losing to linear probes on downstream tasks, DeepMind's deprioritization) narrowed the credible use case to discovering unknown concepts rather than acting on known ones. Likely
  • Causal tools outrank correlational ones: activation patching and steering with controls establish that directions like the refusal direction causally mediate behavior, even while the mechanism computing that behavior remains unexplained — we can steer what we cannot explain. Certain
  • Sleeper-agent persistence, alignment faking, emergent misalignment, and obfuscated reward hacking are replicated experiments; spontaneous scheming in deployment is not among them, and whether the experiments reveal goal-guarding or elicited role-play is genuinely unsettled — interpretability-grade evidence, not scratchpad transcripts, is what would settle it.

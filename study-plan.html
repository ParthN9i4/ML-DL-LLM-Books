A 42-Week Study Plan

Foundations of Modern Machine Learning, paced — with a weekly encrypted-LLM paper track
10–15 hours per week · ~10 months · August 2026

What this is

This schedule paces Foundations of Modern Machine Learning (ml-book.html) and its exercise ladder (ml-foundations/) at one chapter per week, and runs a second, independent track alongside it: one paper per week on homomorphic encryption applied to transformers and large language models, worked through a fixed analysis template.

The two tracks are deliberately not synchronised. The book track builds from linear algebra upward and only reaches encrypted machine learning in Part VIII, around week 38. The paper track starts in week 1. That is intentional: you already work in this area, and the point of the paper track is not to teach you FHE — it is to build a comparison table across forty papers that does not exist anywhere in the literature. By the time the book track arrives at Part VIII, you will have read the primary sources it cites, and you should be reading those chapters critically rather than receptively.

The honest risk with a plan this long

Ten-month schedules fail in month two, and they fail for a specific reason: a missed week becomes a two-week debt, the debt becomes shame, and the shame becomes abandonment. Build the recovery in now. Weeks 11, 22, 33, and 42 are deliberately empty. They are not rest weeks to be reallocated when you feel behind — they are the slack that makes the other 38 weeks survivable. If you arrive at a buffer week on schedule, use it to re-derive something you faked your way past. You will know which one.

The daily shape

Five weekdays at roughly two hours, plus one weekend session for the paper. The structure that matters more than the hours:

  • One 90-minute deep block per day, doing exactly one of reading or implementation — never both in the same block. Switching between them feels productive and is the most reliable way to finish a week having understood nothing.
  • One 30-minute review block, in which you re-derive a single result from the previous day on a blank page, without notes. This is the entire retention mechanism of the plan. If you skip one thing, do not skip this.
  • Write down what you could not derive. Keep one running file. It is the highest-value artifact this plan produces, because it is the only honest record of where your foundations are thin.
The implementation rule

Reading an implementation and writing one are different skills, and only the second one transfers. Every chapter's Artifact is designed to be reconstructible from its specification alone. Read the spec, close the book, write it, run it, and only then compare. When your version differs, the interesting question is never "which is right" — it is why you made the choice you made. That is where the learning is.

The paper-analysis template

Papers in this area are individually impressive and collectively incomparable. They differ in threat model, in encryption scheme, in what they count as preprocessing, in sequence length, in hardware, and in whether the reported latency includes key generation. A summary of each paper is nearly worthless; a table with the same nine columns for forty papers is a literature review.

Extract exactly these, every time, in this order. Where a paper does not report something, write "not reported" rather than omitting the row — the gaps are themselves a finding, and they cluster in revealing places.

The nine points
  1. Threat model. What is hidden, and from whom? Client input only? Model weights? Both? Is the server semi-honest or malicious? How many rounds of interaction, and is the client required to be online throughout? A "private inference" claim means nothing until this row is filled in.
  2. Scheme and parameters. CKKS, BFV/BGV, TFHE/CGGI, or a hybrid with secret sharing or a TEE. Ring dimension $N$, coefficient modulus chain, scale factor, claimed security level in bits, and which standard that claim is against.
  3. Multiplicative depth. How much depth does one transformer layer consume? Where is bootstrapping invoked, if at all — and if the answer is "never", what bounds the number of layers?
  4. The non-polynomial core. How is softmax handled? Normalization (LayerNorm or RMSNorm, and the inverse square root inside it)? The activation (GELU, SiLU, ReLU)? Polynomial approximation, lookup, protocol switch, or client-side round trip — and at what degree and over what input domain.
  5. Packing and slot utilisation. What goes in a ciphertext slot, how are rotations used for the matmuls, and what fraction of slots is actually doing work? This is where most of the real engineering lives and where papers are least forthcoming.
  6. Benchmark conditions. Model and size, sequence length, batch size, hardware (and how many cores or GPUs), and — the row that matters most — what was excluded from the reported time. Key generation, weight encoding, and setup are excluded startlingly often.
  7. Accuracy delta. Against the plaintext model, on which task, at what sequence length. A sub-1% delta on one GLUE task is not evidence about a 7B generative model.
  8. The claim you would attack. Pick one, and name the experiment that would settle it. This is the row that turns reading into research training, and it is the one you will be tempted to skip.
  9. Consequence for your pipeline. Specifically: does this change anything about how you would build CKKS encrypted inference in OpenFHE? A technique you could adopt, a dead end now ruled out, or nothing at all — and "nothing at all" is a legitimate and common answer.

The comparison table is the deliverable

Keep one table, one row per paper, columns 1–7 above. After roughly ten papers it starts answering questions no individual paper does: which depth budgets are actually achievable without bootstrapping, whether anyone has an honest softmax, how much of the reported speedup across five years is scheme improvement versus hardware, and how many "non-interactive" claims survive reading the threat model carefully. Those are survey-paper questions, and a survey is a legitimate first publication.

The weekly automation

A recurring Routine fires once a week, takes the next paper off the queue, fetches it, drafts the nine-point analysis, and diffs it against the previous week's entry on depth, packing, and threat model. The output is a growing comparison table rather than forty isolated summaries.

What the draft is for, and what it is not

The draft exists so that you start from a filled-in table you disagree with rather than a blank one you have to fill. That is a real saving and a real risk. A machine-written analysis is confident in exactly the places a careful reader should not be — it will state a depth budget the paper never gives, and it will accept a latency number without checking what was excluded from it.

So the rule is: the draft is a hypothesis, and your reading is the test. Point 8 — the claim you would attack — is yours alone; do not accept a generated answer for it. If in a given week you only have time either to read the paper or to review the draft, read the paper. The automation is scaffolding for a habit, and scaffolding that replaces the building is worse than none.

If you fall behind

You will. The order of what to cut, when a week compresses:

  1. Cut the crosswalk reading first. One good source beats two skimmed ones.
  2. Then cut the ladder exercises, but never the chapter Artifact. The Artifact is the smallest unit that still forces understanding.
  3. Then drop the paper to a two-week cadence. Half the papers analysed properly beats all of them skimmed — and the comparison table is only as good as its worst row.
  4. Never cut the 30-minute review block. It is 4% of the week and most of the retention.

If you are more than three weeks behind at week 20, the plan is wrong for your life rather than the other way around. Re-pace it to 15 months instead of abandoning it; the schedule is a tool, and a tool that is making you feel bad has stopped working.
